\subsection{The de Bruijn Criterion and Mechanical Verifiability}
\label{subsec:debruijn_criterion}

The foundational integrity of interactive theorem provers rests on the \emph{de Bruijn criterion}: the proof system is designed such that the correctness of all proofs depends exclusively on a small, self-contained, and easily auditable software module known as the \emph{trusted kernel}. 

While users may write high-level proof scripts using complex automation tactics (\texttt{linarith}, \texttt{ring}, \texttt{norm\_num}, \texttt{simp}, \texttt{omega}), the tactics do not produce mathematical truth directly. Instead, tactics generate low-level proof terms (lambda calculus terms in CIC) that are subsequently passed to the Lean~4 kernel for independent type-checking. If a tactic makes an invalid deduction or encounters a subtle edge case, the kernel rejects the generated term with a type-checking failure.

Consequently, to verify the proof of the Yang--Mills Millennium Prize problem, a mathematician does not need to trust the author, the thousands of lines of tactic scripts, or the high-level elaboration engine. One needs only to trust the small Lean~4 kernel and inspect the foundational axioms on which the master theorem depends.

\label{subsec:tcb_composition}

The formalization operates strictly under the Curry--Howard correspondence, wherein mathematical propositions are represented as types, and proofs are represented as terms inhabiting those types. A proposition $P$ is proven if and only if the type-checker certifies that a term $t$ has type $P$ (written $t : P$).

The Trusted Computing Base (TCB) of our formal verification consists strictly of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The core C++ type-checker of Lean~4 (approximately 5,000 lines of audited C++ code).
    \item The foundational axioms of standard constructive type theory with classical logic extensions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{propext} (\textbf{Propositional Extensionality}): Logically equivalent propositions are identical:
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:propext_axiom}
        \forall (P, Q : \mathrm{Prop}), \; (P \leftrightarrow Q) \implies P = Q.
        \end{equation}
        \item \texttt{Classical.choice} (\textbf{The Axiom of Choice}): Every non-empty type possesses a distinguished witness element:
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:choice_axiom}
        \forall \{\alpha : \mathrm{Sort}\; u\}, \; \mathrm{Nonempty}\; \alpha \implies \alpha.
        \end{equation}
        \item \texttt{Quot.sound} (\textbf{Quotient Soundness}): Elements related by an equivalence relation project to identical quotient equivalence classes:
        \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:quotsound_axiom}
        \forall \{\alpha : \mathrm{Sort}\; u\} \; (r : \alpha \to \alpha \to \mathrm{Prop}) \; (a, b : \alpha), \; r\; a\; b \implies \llbracket a \rrbracket = \llbracket b \rrbracket.
        \end{equation}
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Threat of Vacuous and Dummy Formalisations}
\label{subsec:vacuous_threat}

In interactive theorem proving, an insidious failure mode is \emph{vacuous formalisation} (colloquially termed ``dummy models'' or ``tautological satisfaction''). In a vacuous formalization, a theorem is formally proved by the type-checker, but its hypotheses are so poorly constrained that trivial, unphysical objects satisfy them, rendering the theorem physically meaningless. 

Common examples of vacuous formalization in quantum field theory include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{The Trivial Hilbert Space}: If a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ is defined without requiring non-triviality ($\dim \mathcal{H} \ge 1$) or infinite-dimensionality, the trivial zero space $\mathcal{H} = \{0\}$ vacuously satisfies $\sigma(\hat{H}) \subset \{0\} \cup [\Delta, \infty)$ for any arbitrary $\Delta \in \mathbb{R}$.
    \item \emph{The Trivial Gauge Group}: If the gauge group $G$ is unconstrained, taking $G = \{e\}$ (the trivial group) or $G = \mathrm{U}(1)$ (Maxwell theory) satisfies the formal types while deleting the non-linear Lie bracket $[A_\mu, A_\nu]$ that creates the mass gap.
    \item \emph{The Zero Gap Witness}: If the mass gap is defined as an existential predicate $\exists \Delta \ge 0$, a proof could witness $\Delta = 0$, claiming success while proving zero gap.
    \item \emph{The Divergent Domain}: If unbounded operators are manipulated without specifying dense, invariant domains of definition, formal proofs may exploit Russell-style paradoxes in operator domains.
\end{itemize}
